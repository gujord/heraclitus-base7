                                              Abstract
         Starting at zero, the base-seven Heraclitus transform repeatedly chooses an unused
     integer at a power-of-seven distance from its current position, minimizing absolute value
     and preferring the positive integer in a tie. We present a proposed computer-assisted proof
     that this walk visits every integer exactly once. The argument reduces the infinite walk to a
     closed catalogue of normalized boundary states with period 36 across scales. An exact finite
     base and a uniform scale transition yield coverage of [−Ck + 37, Ck − 37] by index 7k , where
     Ck = (7k − 1)/2 and k ≥ 6. The accompanying certificates have a separate Python checker;
     a Lean companion formalizes the exact rule, base, transitions and coverage. Conditional on
     the uniform transition, a first-crossing argument gives lim supn→∞ |a(n)| − n/2 /n ≥ 3/8.
     That complete deviation corollary is not formalized in Lean, and equality remains open.
     This AI-assisted manuscript awaits independent human mathematical review.


Introduction
Greedy walks with restricted jump lengths combine a local choice rule with a global coverage
problem: can a rule that always chooses the unused integer closest to zero eventually visit
every integer? Here the allowed jump lengths are 1, 7, 49, . . .. The challenge is to control the
complete visited set at arbitrarily large scales, including isolated points marked by earlier long
jumps.
Coverage for powers of two already has a public block description [1]. For powers of seven, the
reachable boundary states involve 74- and 130-step local cycles and a 36-phase description
across scales. This makes the base a concrete setting in which to study a finite-state approach
to coverage. The finite closure established here is specific to seven; no result for another odd
base is assumed.
The central mechanism is a uniform marked-endpoint transfer. Translation cycles describe long
stretches of the walk; a finite repair handles the next previsited endpoint. Six such transfers
form each ladder layer. Closing both the entire endpoint state and its spacing residue gives an
induction over arbitrarily many layers, and then over successive scales. A repeating pattern in
sampled prefixes alone would not supply this step.
Section 1 states the exact rule and proposed theorem. Sections 2–7 give the certificate
representation and universal reduction, and Section 8 derives the conditional deviation bound.
Sections 9 and 10 separate executed checks from proof obligations and give reproduction
commands. Sections 11–12 discuss generalization, related work and limitations. The exact
formal rule and coverage statement are reproduced in Section 10.

1    Statement and precise rule
Set a(0) = 0. Having chosen distinct integers a(0), . . . , a(n), choose a(n + 1) among the unused
integers v for which |v − a(n)| is a nonnegative integral power of 7. Minimize |v|, giving positive
v priority in a tie.
There is always an available move: a sufficiently large power reaches outside the finite used set.
Thus the definition gives an infinite sequence without repetitions.
Theorem 1 (coverage). Every integer occurs in a. More precisely, put Ck = (7k − 1)/2. For
every k ≥ 6,

                            [−Ck + 37, Ck − 37] ⊆ {a(0), . . . , a(7k )}.                        (1)

The complete state at n = 7k , translated by Ck separately on each side of zero, has period 36
in k for k ≥ 6. The direct initial check also gives the same phase table at k = 4, 5.
For a fixed integer z choose k large enough that |z| ≤ Ck − 37. Then (1) gives an occurrence
of z. Uniqueness follows from the rule. This last implication is elementary. The substantive
work is proving the finite state transition uniformly for arbitrarily large k.


2    States and certificates
A normalized half-line state is (c, B). All coordinates below 0 are used or forbidden, 0 is unused,
B is a finite set of positive used coordinates, and the current coordinate is c. Thus c < 0
or c belongs to B. After a greedy choice v, adjoin v, let d be the least missing nonnegative
coordinate, and subtract d from all coordinates. The new state is

                              v − d, {x − d : x ∈ B ∪ {v}, x ≥ d} .                              (2)
                                                                     


and the front advances by d. Greedy here means the least unused half-line neighbor, not least
absolute value on the signed line.
The checker verifies a transition by scanning candidate integers 0, 1, . . . , v and testing whether
the absolute distance is a power of 7 by repeated integer division. Consequently every smaller
possible candidate is excluded, including candidates using larger powers. It does not import
the generator’s neighbor-selection function.
In a signed state at scale m = 7k , write C = (m − 1)/2. A point on side s ∈ {+1, −1} has the
form s(C + x). A side record (f, B) means that every positive magnitude below C + f is used,
C + f is unused, and the additional used magnitudes are C + x for x in B. B contains only
values greater than f . The current record is (s, x). Zero is always used. These records specify
the entire visited set, not just a bounded sample of it.
The accompanying JSON files contain every finite transition needed below, including complete
transient and cycle words. They are part of the computer-assisted proof. A separate, self-
contained verifier checks the stated identities and greedy choices.

Wi = Di + vi , for 0 ≤ i < P . Suppose the Wi give distinct residues modulo P . Then successive
cycles choose Wi + qP , q ≥ 0, relative to the front at cycle entry. The certificates check all
these finite hypotheses for the cycles used below.
Proof. After the first block the prefix below the new front is filled, the finite pattern B is
the same, and the current point has the same relative coordinate. Distances and order of
candidates are translation invariant. Backward jumps below the front land in the filled prefix.
Induction therefore repeats the identical normalized block. This is an infinite statement derived
from the finite transition checks.
Lemma 3 (uniform marked-endpoint transfer). Work relative to a completed marked
endpoint at coordinate 0. Let (f, c, B), with f > 0, describe a valid half-line state: every
coordinate below f is used, f is unused, the remaining used points are f + B, and the current
point f + c is used. Add a next previsited endpoint h ≥ 2401 and any finite set of later
previsited endpoints, all at least 2h. Assume that the walk without these additional endpoints
has a checked finite transient followed by a cycle satisfying Lemma 2. Assume also that the
marker insertion and finite repair described below are checked exact half-line transitions and
satisfy the five bounds below. Then the returned finite endpoint record, measured relative to
h, depends only on the initial state and h modulo P . The complete used set consists of that
record and its filled prefix, together with the untouched later endpoints. Those later endpoints
do not affect the transfer choices or its finite output record.
Proof and finite hypotheses. Temporarily ignore the additional endpoints and follow the
unmarked half-line walk. Let f∗ be the front at entry to its translation cycle of length P .
There is exactly one cycle phase i with

                                  h − f∗ − Wi ≡ 0    (mod P ).

If q = (h − f∗ − Wi )/P ≥ 0, the unmarked process first selects h at that phase of cycle q.
Declaring h used from the beginning changes none of the preceding choices: until a removed
candidate would have won, removing it does not change the minimum. At this instant insert
the marker, without moving the current point or counting a new choice, normalize by (2), and
continue until the front is strictly beyond h. This defines the next endpoint state.
At a fixed phase i, the unmarked normalized state is the same in every cycle, and the inserted
marker has normalized coordinate vi . Thus its normalized insertion state and finite repair
word are independent of q; the repair can be checked once for each input state and spacing
residue.

                              A + 6 · 7j , A + 5 · 7j , . . . , A + 7 j .

filled prefix. Smaller backward jumps leave a larger candidate than subtraction of 7j . The
indicated points are unused. Forward jumps are larger. Since |A| < 100 and 7j ≥ 2401, no
ordering ambiguity arises.

5    The signed boundary rule, including all larger powers
Let M be a large power of 7 and CM = (M − 1)/2. Suppose that on each side the entire used
set is described by a boundary record with holes and current coordinate within 512 of CM . In
these coordinates a cross-side jump of length M is the reflection

                                      (s, x) 7−→ (−s, 1 − x).                                    (4)

The finite boundary process offers all unused same-side power neighbors and the unused
reflected point. It orders the candidates by their offset, preferring side + in a tie.
Lemma 5 (signed boundary rule). For M ≥ 76 , with the complete used-set records and the
bounds just specified, the finite boundary rule agrees with the signed greedy rule throughout
each certified boundary word. Proof. Any power below M is at most M/7. It cannot cross
zero from CM + x with |x| < 512. If such a backward jump is too large to reach the bounded
boundary region, it lands in the filled prefix; the corresponding forward jump is worse than
the finite candidate supplied by the certificate. The jump M is exactly (4). Any power at
least 7M reaches a magnitude far larger than an available boundary candidate. The numerical
margin M > 100000 is more than sufficient for all these inequalities.
The separate checker scans offsets in increasing order, testing side + before side -, instead of
using the generator’s neighbor enumeration. It checks that every current point and chosen
point, and each filled-front coordinate, stays within the stated bound.

7    Closure for all exponents and coverage
All translation periods used in Section 6 divide L = 19240. All layer cycles in Section 4 divide
36 and have stabilized by height 5. By (3), replacing k by k + 36 leaves m modulo every
required period unchanged. Equations (4)-(6), the first-crossing inequalities, and the endpoint
transfer rule consequently give the same normalized transition for every k in a given phase.
All size restrictions are preserved as k increases. The common bound m ≥ 76 ensures the
finitely many initial size restrictions.
The verifier checks 36 such transitions, with complete inputs and outputs, and checks that
they close in order. This establishes a finite transition rule, not a comparison of two sampled
orbits.
For the base case a separate uncompressed implementation generates every term from n = 0

    ∀ v : Int, (¬∃ i : Nat, i≤n ∧ a i=v) →
      (∃ j : Nat, (v-a n).natAbs=7^j) →
      (a (n+1)).natAbs≤v.natAbs ∧
      ((a (n+1)).natAbs=v.natAbs → v≤a (n+1))

The coverage theorem’s declaration is:
theorem coverage (a : Nat → Int) (rule : FollowsRule a) :
    ∀ x : Int, ∃ i : Nat, a i=x ∧ ∀ j : Nat, a j=x → j=i

Here Nat contains zero, so the allowed exponent includes j = 0. The last inequality gives
the larger integer, hence the positive integer, priority when absolute values tie. The theorem
canonical_follows_rule proves that the canonical sequence satisfies this rule. Statement
fidelity and the universal reduction in Sections 3–7 remain the main targets for mathematical
review.

11     Generalization and limitations
The marked-point argument is not specific to seven: for any fixed base, a certified translation
cycle gives a transfer depending on the marker spacing modulo its period. For an odd base
b, the coordinates C = (bk − 1)/2 are integral, the boundary reflection is again x 7→ 1 − x,
the high descent creates b − 1 endpoints per layer, and the two boundary scales differ by
(b − 1)bk /2. Thus the proof architecture extends naturally to other odd bases.
What does not automatically extend is the existence of a finite closed catalogue of entrance
states and marker transfers. In an unrestricted experiment, inserting arbitrary extra markers
into all discovered base-seven cycles generated many additional cycles and did not close before
the imposed 200000-state limit. The proof uses the 119 transfers forced by its particular
ladders and scale states, not arbitrary marker patterns. This restriction must be retained in
any generalization.
An auxiliary check shows that a single premarked point in an otherwise clean base-seven
half-line walk with incoming distance 1 through 7 always allows eventual local coverage.
Its reduction has 22 marker phases and 690 normalized states, ending in cycles of lengths
1,4,8,74,130. This explains the earlier appearances of 74 and 130, but is not itself the global
signed proof.
The priority for another base should be to identify its reachable marker transfers and prove a
finite closure, or replace that closure by a uniform hole bound. Additional large prefixes alone
do not establish it. The sharp 3/8 deviation claim requires a separate argument about the
order inside scales and is not claimed.


12     Earlier work and possible novelty
The definition and the coverage problem belong to the existing Heraclitus-transform literature
and OEIS work. OEIS A379719 gives coverage and a block description for powers of two [1];
OEIS A383442 asks a broader coverage question [2]. Sloane’s 2025 slides discuss a general
infinite-step-set/gcd criterion [4, slide 8]. In his Rutgers Experimental Math Seminar on 10
September 2026, he formulates the conjecture that the Heraclitus transform of a set S of
positive step sizes visits every integer if and only if S is infinite and gcd(S) = 1 [8]. The
2026 slides also express doubt about sufficiency [8, slide 9]. Here S = {7j : j ≥ 0} is infinite
and contains 1, so it satisfies both conditions. The present coverage argument addresses this
particular set; it provides no conclusion for arbitrary step sets. None of these general ideas is
claimed as new here.
OEIS A377091 announces, on 29 October 2026, an AI-assisted proof for square steps under
review [3]. That announcement does not describe a powers-of-seven theorem. No manuscript
for that proof was obtained during this investigation, so possible overlap with Sloane, Xausa,
Karlsson or collaborators cannot be ruled out.
Searches on 2 November 2026 covered the transform’s name, powers of seven, odd bases, the
periods 74 and 130, the signed initial prefix, the relevant OEIS entries, and the authors’ names.
Sloane’s home page and publication list were also inspected [5, 6], and the relevant 2025 slide
was read and visually checked. No matching prior base-seven coverage result was found in the
sources searched. Possible overlap with unpublished work remains unresolved. Search engines
returned much unrelated philosophical material; this is a limitation, not positive evidence of
novelty. No absence-of-results search proves priority.
A bounded follow-up search on 3 November 2026 queried the OEIS JSON API for the signed
initial prefix and for the phrases “not yet in the sequence” together with “power of 3”, “power
